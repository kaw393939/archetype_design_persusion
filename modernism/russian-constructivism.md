# Russian Constructivism
[Back to this section](README.md) · [Home](../README.md)

## What is it?
Russian Constructivism was an avant-garde movement that developed around the 1917 Russian Revolution, with roots around 1915 and major activity into the early 1930s. Artists including Vladimir Tatlin, Aleksandr Rodchenko, Varvara Stepanova, and El Lissitzky argued that art should serve society, production, and industry. They moved away from art made mainly for decoration and applied their ideas to posters, books, exhibitions, clothing, and useful objects.

Graphic design became an important public form of the movement. Constructivist designers used geometry, photography, and forceful lettering to communicate quickly. Their work appeared in political campaigns and in state commercial advertising during the New Economic Policy, which began in 1921. Lissitzky also worked in contact with Kazimir Malevich and Suprematism. His designs sometimes combine Suprematist abstract forms with direct political text.

## How do you recognize or use it?
Common features include strong diagonals, flat circles and triangles, photomontage, and a limited palette led by red, black, and white. Bold sans-serif lettering often becomes part of the geometry instead of sitting quietly above or below an image. Scale changes sharply, and elements point toward a message or action. These choices give a composition speed, tension, and a sense of collective purpose.

For a current design, use the style when the message needs urgency or confident direction. Keep the composition controlled. A few decisive shapes will communicate the idea better than many decorative details. Photographs can be cropped tightly and placed at an angle, while type can follow the same directional structure.

### How to use it in a hero
Build the hero around a diagonal layout with red, black, and white blocks. Set the headline as part of the geometry, then place the CTA inside a solid block on the dominant diagonal. Maintain strong contrast and enough clear space for the action to remain easy to find.

## Examples
### El Lissitzky, *Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge*
Created in 1920, this color lithograph was printed in red and black by the Litizdat branch of the Political Directorate of the Western Front during the Russian Civil War. The Russian title is *Klinom krasnym bei belykh*. A red wedge represents the Bolsheviks piercing a white circle that represents the anti-Bolshevik White forces. The work reduces a political conflict to a sharp geometric action. Copies are held by the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston and Tate, whose copy was purchased in 2016 as part of the David King Collection. [MFA record](https://collections.mfa.org/objects/314484/klinom-krasnym-bei-belykh-beat-the-whites-with-the-red-wedg) and [Tate description](https://shop.tate.org.uk/lissitzky-beat-the-whites-with-the-red-wedge/redsta1717.html).

### Aleksandr Rodchenko, *Dobrolet (Poster for a Russian state airline)*
Rodchenko made this lithograph in 1923 for Dobrolet, a state airline. Under the NEP, he designed the airline's posters, logo, and stationery. The aircraft crosses the design diagonally, suggesting movement and new technology. Institution: The Museum of Modern Art, New York. [MoMA record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/7269). Related stationery is held by The Metropolitan Museum of Art: [The Met record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/816045).
