# Commitment and Consistency
[Back to this section](README.md) · [Home](../README.md)

## What is it?
Commitment and consistency is a persuasion principle described by Robert Cialdini. People often prefer to act in ways that match what they have already said or done. A prior choice can become a guide for a later decision, especially when the first commitment was active, public, and voluntary.

The principle helps explain the foot-in-the-door technique. A person first agrees to a small request, then later receives a related larger request. Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser studied this pattern in 1966. In one part of their research, homeowners who first agreed to a small request were much more likely to agree to a larger request later. The earlier action created a relevant commitment without requiring the researchers to use pressure.

## How do you recognize or use it?
A commitment can be an expressed preference, a saved choice, a written plan, or a small completed action. In a product flow, a shopper might choose a fit, save a size, or build a short list before deciding to buy. The first action should connect naturally to the later one. A random task may create activity, but it does not create a meaningful path toward the purchase.

Useful commitment steps give the person something of value immediately. Saving a size can make a return visit easier. Choosing a preferred fit can narrow the product details shown next. The interface should also make it simple to revise or remove the choice, since consistency should support decision-making rather than punish a change of mind.

Ethics: the first step should be honest and useful on its own; do not trap people. State what will happen after a click, avoid preselected consent, and do not hide a purchase behind an apparently harmless action.

## Examples
### Hypothetical example for our team's practice brand

Headline: Find your fit for the everyday white tee.

Supporting line: Choose your preferred cut and save your size for a simpler product check.

CTA: "Pick my fit" goes to a fit selector with straight and relaxed options, followed by an optional size-saving step.

Choosing a fit is a small, active decision that is useful before any purchase. When the shopper later reaches the product page, the selected cut and saved size provide a consistent next step toward adding the shirt to the cart. The choice remains editable, and completing the selector does not enroll the shopper or place an order.
