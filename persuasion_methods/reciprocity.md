# The Reciprocity Principle

The Reciprocity Principle is one of Robert Cialdini's seven universal principles of influence. It describes the tendency for people to feel an obligation to give back when they have first received a behavior, gift, service, or favor from someone else. In persuasion, reciprocity can make a request more acceptable because the recipient may feel a social obligation to respond in kind.

Reciprocity does not mean that every gift or favor will automatically produce compliance. The context, relationship, value of what is given, and way it is presented can all affect how people respond. Cialdini emphasizes that effective use of reciprocity involves giving first, with the gift or favor being useful, personalized, and unexpected.

## Why reciprocity influences decisions

People commonly recognize a social expectation to return favors and benefits they receive from others. This expectation can help maintain cooperation and relationships because people are generally reluctant to receive benefits without giving something back.

Research has demonstrated that a small favor can influence a person's willingness to comply with a later request. In a laboratory experiment, Regan (1971) found that participants who received a small favor were more likely to comply with a subsequent request. The findings suggested that normative pressure to reciprocate, rather than simply liking the person who provided the favor, helped explain the relationship between the favor and compliance.

Reciprocity can therefore influence decisions by creating a sense of social obligation. However, using a gift or favor to pressure someone into an unwanted decision can become manipulative.

## When to use it

Reciprocity can be appropriate when the initial benefit provides genuine value to the person receiving it.

Appropriate uses include:

- Providing useful information before asking someone to take action.
- Offering a free sample, resource, or trial that allows people to experience a product or service.
- Providing helpful customer support before requesting feedback or continued engagement.
- Contributing time or assistance to a team before asking colleagues for assistance.
- Giving a meaningful and relevant resource to a community before inviting participation.

Poor-fit uses include:

- Giving something while hiding an important obligation.
- Using a gift to pressure someone into purchasing something they do not want.
- Making the recipient feel guilty for not responding to a favor.
- Providing something of little or no value solely to create an obligation.
- Using reciprocity to disguise deceptive or misleading information.

## How to apply it

### Imagery

Images can communicate the value of what is being offered before a request is made. For example, a campaign offering a free educational resource might show the resource being used by the intended audience.

The imagery should accurately represent what people will receive. Showing a benefit that is unavailable or substantially different from the actual offer can undermine trust.

### Color

Color can be used to make a free resource, sample, or benefit visually noticeable. The design should make the offer easy to understand without making the recipient feel pressured.

Color should support the message rather than create a false sense of urgency or importance.

### Typography

Typography should make the initial benefit and any later request clear. Headings and emphasis can help users distinguish between what is being offered and what is being requested in return.

Important conditions should not be hidden through very small text or confusing formatting.

### Persuasive phrasing

Persuasive language can communicate what is being provided before presenting a request.

Examples include:

- "Here is a free guide to help you get started."
- "Try a sample before you decide."
- "We have prepared this resource for you."
- "Thank you for participating. If you found this useful, consider sharing your feedback."

The language should not imply that accepting a free item creates an unavoidable obligation to purchase, donate, or agree.

## Example: A small unexpected favor

A classic example of reciprocity comes from research involving restaurant diners. Cialdini describes studies in which diners received a small gift, such as a mint, near the end of a meal. The research demonstrated that the gift could affect tipping behavior, with the effect varying according to how the gift was presented.

This example illustrates an important feature of reciprocity: the initial benefit occurs before the recipient makes the later decision. It also shows that the way a benefit is presented can influence its effect.

A separate laboratory experiment by Regan (1971) found that participants who received a small favor were more likely to comply with a later request. The study suggested that normative pressure to reciprocate helped explain the relationship between receiving a favor and subsequent compliance.

## Practical applications

### Free resources

Organizations can provide useful guides, checklists, templates, or educational materials before asking people to participate in a program or provide feedback.

### Product samples

A business can allow potential customers to try a product before asking them to purchase it. The sample should be presented honestly and without implying that receiving it creates a mandatory obligation to buy.

### Customer service

Providing meaningful assistance before asking a customer for feedback or continued engagement can create a positive reciprocal relationship.

### Teamwork

Reciprocity can also operate in professional settings. A team member who willingly provides assistance to a colleague may contribute to a culture in which colleagues are more willing to assist one another.

### Community participation

Organizations can provide useful services, information, or resources to a community before inviting community members to participate in an initiative.

## Brands and campaigns

Examples of reciprocity in marketing can include free product samples, complimentary resources, introductory services, and other benefits provided before a request is made.

These examples should be understood as illustrative applications of the reciprocity principle and not as evidence that a particular organization intentionally designed its strategy around Cialdini's principle unless the organization's strategy has been documented.

Cialdini's discussion of restaurant tipping provides a research-based example of how a small unexpected benefit can influence subsequent behavior. His work also discusses free samples as an application of reciprocity.

## Ethical considerations

Reciprocity should be used in a way that respects the person's ability to make an independent decision.

Ethical use includes:

- Giving something that has genuine value.
- Clearly communicating what is being offered.
- Avoiding deceptive conditions.
- Avoiding guilt or excessive pressure.
- Making requests proportionate to what was provided.
- Allowing people to decline without punishment or embarrassment.
- Maintaining honesty throughout the interaction.

Cialdini's approach emphasizes ethical application of the principles of influence. Reciprocity should help establish cooperation rather than create an unfair or hidden obligation.
