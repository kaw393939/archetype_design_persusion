# The Unity Principle

The Unity Principle is one of Robert Cialdini's methods of persuasion. It describes how people can be more receptive to someone or something they experience as part of “us.” Unity is more than liking a person or agreeing with a message: it comes from a sense of shared identity, membership, or belonging. A brand can invite people into that feeling, but it cannot create genuine unity simply by declaring that everyone already belongs.

## Why unity influences decisions

People often use group membership to understand who they are, what matters to them, and whom they can trust. A shared identity can make a recommendation feel relevant because it comes from someone perceived as “one of us.” It can also make a purchase feel expressive: choosing a product may signal participation in a community or commitment to its values.

Belonging can be meaningful, but it is not automatically a reason to buy. A campaign that recognizes a real community, shared practice, or common experience can help people feel seen. A campaign that invents sameness, pressures people to prove membership, or treats outsiders with contempt can instead feel false or coercive.

## When to use it

Unity is a good fit when a brand has a credible connection to a community, shared interest, common experience, or cause. It can support products made for a particular activity, campaigns built with community members, or services that help people take part in something together. It is especially useful when customers already identify with the practice or values being represented.

Unity is a poor fit when the audience has no meaningful connection to the proposed identity, when the brand claims a community's voice without listening to its members, or when belonging is made conditional on buying. Avoid implying that a product makes someone a better member of a group or that people outside the group are less worthy.

## How to apply it

**Imagery.** Show real people taking part in the shared activity: a neighborhood clean-up, a group ride, a family meal, or a club's weekly gathering. Use varied, recognizable participants and settings rather than treating one person as the universal face of a community. When possible, work with community members and obtain informed permission for their stories and images. A posed group photo can suggest connection, but candid details of the shared activity make the connection more credible.

**Color.** Start with the brand's established palette and the community's actual visual context. For a neighborhood or outdoor campaign, forest green `#2F5D50` can suggest care for shared places, while clear sky blue `#4A90A4` can add openness; warm white `#F5F3EE` and charcoal `#252525` keep product details legible. These are adaptable starting points, not universal symbols of belonging. Use a group's colors, emblems, or cultural patterns only with permission and understanding, and check color contrast for readability.

**Typography.** Choose a typeface that fits the community and remains easy to read. A sturdy humanist sans serif can feel approachable for a neighborhood initiative; a familiar serif can suit an intergenerational or local-history story. Pair a distinctive headline face with a legible body face, and do not imitate a group's lettering or language in ways that turn its identity into decoration. Keep names, dates, and participation details clear.

**Persuasive phrasing.** Use specific, invitational language that names what people actually share. For example: “Made for the Saturday morning ride” or “Join neighbors keeping the river trail clean.” Use “we” only when the brand is genuinely participating or speaking with the community's permission. Avoid lines such as “real locals wear this” or “if you care, you'll buy one”; those turn belonging into a test and attach it to a transaction.

## Example: an ordinary white T-shirt

A basic white T-shirt can support Unity without pretending the shirt itself creates community. A local running club, campus volunteer group, or neighborhood arts collective could offer it as an optional shared uniform for gatherings. The group might invite members to contribute a small design on the inside label, choose the shirt together, or wear it at an annual event. The product then marks participation in a real shared activity rather than conferring membership on whoever pays.

Show actual members wearing the shirt while doing something the group genuinely does, with permission and representation that reflects the group. Keep the shirt's fit, fabric, price, and care details visible. Copy could read: “For the people who meet at the park at 7 a.m. Bring your own shirt or wear ours; every runner is welcome.” If proceeds support a group, state the amount or share and how it will be used. Do not imply that buying the shirt is required to join, that every member agrees on a design, or that the group endorses the brand beyond what it has approved.

## Practical applications

**Shared identity.** A bookstore features local readers recommending books in their own words and invites customers to a free monthly reading circle. The campaign gives the community room to speak instead of claiming that one genre or purchase defines a “real” reader.

**Community participation.** A grocery store partners with neighborhood growers for a seasonal market. It names the growers, explains their role, and helps shoppers meet them. The shared local connection is visible and verifiable rather than an unsupported “shop local” slogan.

**Common experiences.** A travel company publishes travelers' stories about navigating a first solo trip, including ordinary challenges and practical tips. People with that experience may recognize themselves in the stories without the company implying that all solo travelers are alike.

**Group belonging.** A sports retailer supports an adaptive cycling club by providing a place to meet and equipment demonstrations led by participants. The club decides how it is represented, and the campaign makes participation accessible to newcomers instead of presenting the group as a closed circle.

## Brands and campaigns

These examples show ways public campaigns can make shared identity or participation visible. They are illustrative applications of Unity, not evidence that a brand's internal strategy was based on Cialdini's principle.

**Apple “Shot on iPhone.”** The campaign features photographs and videos made by iPhone users, presenting customers as a broad community of creators rather than only as an audience for product claims. The shared practice is making and sharing images; the work of individual contributors gives that identity substance. [Apple, Shot on iPhone](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2022/04/apple-celebrates-the-best-photos-from-the-shot-on-iphone-macro-challenge/)

**Nike “Equality.”** Nike's campaign used sport and athlete voices to communicate a public message about equality and respect. It connects the brand with people who see fair participation as a shared value, while its effectiveness depends on whether the company's actions support the message. [Nike News, Equality](https://about.nike.com/en/newsroom/releases/nike-equality-campaign)

**LEGO Ideas.** LEGO Ideas invites fans to submit concepts, support other builders' projects, and potentially see selected fan designs developed as products. The platform's community is built around a concrete shared activity: imagining, building, and giving feedback. Participation is more than a slogan because members have visible ways to contribute. [LEGO Ideas](https://ideas.lego.com/)

## Ethical considerations

Do not fabricate belonging. A brand should not claim to represent a community, use its symbols, or tell its members' stories without meaningful involvement and permission. Avoid presenting a diverse group as a single voice, and do not suggest that a product purchase proves someone's identity, values, or loyalty.

Do not create manipulative exclusion. Unity should invite people into shared participation, not stigmatize outsiders or manufacture an “us versus them” conflict to drive sales. Make eligibility, costs, and participation requirements clear, and provide accessible ways to join that do not depend on buying merchandise. If a campaign supports a community, be precise about what support is offered and let participants correct how they are represented.
